Cambridge AS Level Computer Science (9618)

Everything you need to know about the course and the exam before you start the learning journey.

1. The big picture

The AS Level is the first half of the full Cambridge International A Level in Computer Science. It is examined by two written papers. Both are answered on the question paper, with no calculator. Together they cover 12 syllabus sections, and the two papers carry equal weight.

2. The two papers

Paper 1: Theory Fundamentals (9618/1x)

  • 1 hour 30 minutes, 75 marks
  • Short-answer and structured questions, all compulsory
  • Covers syllabus sections 1 to 8
  • You write your answers on the question paper
  • Mostly knowledge, explanation and short calculations (binary, hexadecimal, file sizes, logic circuits, SQL)

Paper 2: Fundamental Problem-solving and Programming Skills (9618/2x)

  • 2 hours, 75 marks
  • Structured questions, all compulsory
  • Covers syllabus sections 9 to 12
  • You get an insert with the list of pseudocode functions and operators, and sometimes a program or scenario to refer to
  • Mostly writing, reading and tracing pseudocode, plus design and testing questions

The code in the paper number tells you the variant: 11, 12 and 13 are Paper 1 variants, and 21, 22 and 23 are Paper 2 variants. Different variants contain different questions with the same structure and difficulty. Exams are held in the May/June and October/November series.

4. The kinds of question you will meet

  • State / give / identify / name: a short fact or term. One mark per correct point.
  • Describe / outline: say what something is or how it works, usually in two or three marks' worth of points.
  • Explain / justify: give a point and the reason. A bare statement earns no mark.
  • Complete a table or diagram: ticks in a table, trace tables, logic truth tables, a circuit, a state diagram, or filling in missing words.
  • Calculate or convert: binary, hexadecimal, two's complement, file sizes. Show your working.
  • Write or complete pseudocode (Paper 2): the biggest mark totals in the exam, often 6 to 12 marks per question.
  • Trace or correct an algorithm: follow the code line by line, or find and fix the error.
  • Write SQL (Paper 1): create a table or query and update data using the SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE statements.

5. How you are marked

Examiners follow a published mark scheme. It is a list of specific points (keywords and ideas). You earn one mark for each correct point you make, up to the maximum for that part. There are no marks for length.

  • Correct technical words matter. Vague everyday language ("it's faster", "it does the work") usually earns nothing.
  • Some marks have a cap: for example, an answer must mention the given context to earn full marks.
  • Pseudocode is marked on whether each required step is present, not on perfect spelling or layout.
  • This site marks your past-paper answers the same way: by the mark-scheme keywords. After you answer, you always see the real published mark scheme.

6. How this learning journey works

  • Every lesson: concept, worked example, quick checks, then a real past-paper question in exam style.
  • Lessons unlock in order within each paper. Paper 1 and Paper 2 are two separate tracks, so you can work on either at any time.
  • Your answer is marked against the mark scheme. Then you see the exact published scheme and what the examiners said about common mistakes.
  • Below 60% of the marks, the lesson is flagged "review later". You can always move on, but come back to it.
Exam technique. Read how many marks a part is worth and give that many distinct points. Use the technical word the syllabus uses. In Paper 2, read the insert first, name variables clearly and never leave a pseudocode question blank: partial credit is common.

The syllabus document and the mark schemes are the final authority, so check the latest Cambridge syllabus for your exam year.
